Reading Guide

AI for Weather: From Weather Forecasting to Earth System Prediction

The fundamental task of weather forecasting is to project the future from the current state of the atmosphere and oceans. A modern meteorological operation typically consists of observing systems, data assimilation, numerical models and forecast verification: satellites, radars and ground stations continuously record the state of the Earth system; data assimilation fuses these observations into an initial field; numerical models solve the dynamical and physical processes; and ensemble forecasting further characterizes the uncertainty of the future. Nowcasting focuses on severe convection and precipitation over the next few hours; medium-range forecasting covers days to two weeks; subseasonal prediction extends to several weeks; and climate simulation studies the statistical response to external forcing over much longer timescales.

In recent years, AI has gradually entered multiple links of this operational chain, with several technical routes developing in parallel. Data-driven models learn the evolution of historical weather fields and deliver global forecasts at far lower computational cost; generative models and ensemble learning describe uncertainty; hybrid models couple neural networks with dynamical equations; data assimilation models attempt to connect directly to real observations; and ocean and climate models target Earth system prediction at different timescales. This course selects 26 representative papers across six sections, aiming to help us understand what problems AI-for-Weather research is solving, which key methods it employs, and how these routes jointly expand the research landscape of Earth system prediction.

Current research has formed a landscape covering observation, analysis, forecasting, ensemble generation and evaluation. Six keywords capture this process: global-field modeling, probabilization, physics integration, cross-scale modeling, observation-driven methods, and trustworthy evaluation. These directions intersect and jointly determine whether AI can genuinely enter the Earth system forecasting pipeline.

01

Global Weather Forecasting

8 papers

Global weather forecasting predicts the evolution of the atmosphere over the coming days to two weeks from its current global state. A model must simultaneously handle temperature, wind, humidity and pressure at different altitudes, and capture how weather systems propagate around the globe. The early global machine-learning weather models selected here are dominated by deterministic forecasters: FourCastNet, Pangu-Weather, GraphCast and FuXi explore frequency-domain operators, 3D atmospheric structures, spherical graphs and lead-time cascades respectively. GenCast and FuXi-ENS further incorporate probabilistic modeling and ensemble quality into training, extending global probabilistic forecasting to high-impact weather such as tropical cyclones.

Section Summary

Global AI weather forecasting has expanded from a single deterministic trajectory to probabilistic ensembles and probability products for high-impact weather. Moving from deterministic forecasters to direct probabilistic modeling changes not only the number of outputs, but also the training objective and how forecast quality is measured. The design trade-offs of different models can be understood along three threads: global-field representation, ensemble generation, and cross-task transfer.

Discussion

  • Which source of error is addressed respectively by frequency-domain operators, 3D transformers, spherical graphs and cascaded models?
  • Should the value of a probabilistic model be measured by mean error, ensemble spread, calibration, or gains on extreme events?
  • When does the shared representation of a foundation model help weather forecasting, and when might it mask task specificity?

02

Precipitation Nowcasting

5 papers

Precipitation nowcasting typically targets the next 0–6 hours, predicting the movement, growth, decay and dissipation of precipitation systems from sequences of radar echoes. Early deep-learning methods treated radar extrapolation as deterministic video prediction, often producing overly smooth results. DGMR uses a generative model to produce multiple plausible futures; EarthFormer improves the general space-time representation. NowcastNet then introduces physical evolution into the generative model, while PreDiff and DiffCast handle stochastic evolution along different diffusion-modeling routes. The field has thus formed two main threads: one improves the space-time architecture, and the other redistributes the roles of data-driven learning, physical constraints and probabilistic generation.

Section Summary

Precipitation nowcasting is shifting from predicting one future radar image to describing a set of possible convective evolutions. A model must simultaneously preserve motion continuity, local intensity and probabilistic diversity. The space-time architecture determines how precipitation motion is organized, while physical constraints and probabilistic generation help characterize intensity changes and stochastic detail.

Discussion

  • What assumptions about uncertainty underlie DGMR's diversity, NowcastNet's physical division of labor, and DiffCast's residual decomposition?
  • Should domain knowledge enter through the inputs, the network structure, the generation guidance, or the evaluation metrics?
  • For extreme precipitation, under what circumstances might visual realism and warning value diverge?

03

Subseasonal Forecast & Climate Simulation

4 papers

Subseasonal forecasting targets atmospheric change over two weeks to two months, attempting to extract slower signals — such as the monsoon, the MJO and air-sea coupled modes — from the rapidly decaying predictability of weather. Climate simulation and projection focus instead on multi-year statistics and how external forcing such as greenhouse gases alters the climate system. At these timescales AI has taken on multiple task forms: FuXi-S2S directly generates 42-day ensemble forecasts; QuickClim rapidly emulates and reconstructs multivariate climate responses along prescribed CO₂e concentration pathways; NeuralGCM couples a differentiable dynamical core with learned physics parameterizations; and UniCM focuses on the coupling between climate modes such as ENSO.

Section Summary

The longer the timescale, the more the research object shifts from instantaneous weather fields to low-frequency signals, ensemble distributions and climate statistics. Subseasonal forecasting looks for signals in the initial state that persist for weeks, while climate projection concerns the statistical response under given external forcing. Understanding the information sources of each task helps clarify the respective modeling philosophies of direct prediction, hybrid dynamics and climate emulation.

Discussion

  • Should accuracy mean the same thing for weather forecasting, subseasonal forecasting and climate simulation?
  • In which scenarios does NeuralGCM's hybrid structure outperform purely data-driven models?
  • Can the attention or coupling structures in climate-mode prediction be interpreted as evidence of dynamical mechanisms?

04

AI Ocean Forecasting

3 papers

The ocean stores and transports vast amounts of heat, momentum and matter, exerting a sustained influence on weather and climate. Ocean forecasting must estimate sea-surface height, temperature, salinity and currents while coping with eddies, boundary currents, air-sea exchange and sparse observations. Compared with the atmosphere, the ocean evolves more slowly, carries longer memory, and offers far more limited three-dimensional observations. Current ocean AI models unfold across different spatial and temporal scales: OceanNet focuses on regional sea-surface height, WenHai predicts the global upper eddying ocean, and ORCA-DL targets the global three-dimensional upper ocean at monthly scale.

Section Summary

The key to intelligent ocean forecasting is balancing local eddy detail, three-dimensional structure, air-sea forcing and long-term stability. Atmospheric forcing introduces momentum, heat and freshwater exchange into ocean prediction, while multivariate modeling captures the links among temperature, salinity and currents. When reading, focus on how each model represents the ocean's internal evolution and how it receives external driving at the air-sea interface.

Discussion

  • How do regional boundary conditions affect the predictions of an FNO-based ocean digital twin?
  • What is the difference between providing air-sea fluxes as inputs and explicitly coupling an atmospheric model?
  • For long-term stable integration, how should one distinguish numerical non-divergence from climatological credibility?

05

Benchmarks & Trustworthy Evaluation

3 papers

Model comparison is meaningfully interpretable only when data, initialization, variable definitions, forecast lead times, spatial regridding, climatological baselines and evaluation metrics are as consistent as possible. Weather forecasting also exhibits clear regional, variable and lead-time differences: a model may excel in global mean error yet lose its advantage on local gales or extreme heat. Benchmarks must therefore establish unified evaluation protocols and extend average skill to probabilistic quality and extreme events.

Section Summary

Benchmarks determine how we understand model progress. Unified data and metrics answer whether models can be compared fairly; extreme-event evaluation further answers whether a model genuinely improves high-impact weather forecasting. Deterministic error, probabilistic calibration and extreme-event performance together form a multidimensional evaluation of forecast capability.

Discussion

  • Among a benchmark's data, baselines, metrics and implementation code, which part most easily changes the conclusion?
  • Can deterministic models and probabilistic ensemble models share the same leaderboard?
  • Should extreme-event evaluation center on thresholds, return periods, object identification, or decision loss?

06

Data Assimilation & Analysis-Forecast Coupling

3 papers

Weather forecasting first requires knowing what state the atmosphere is in right now. Satellite, radar, surface-station and sounding observations are unevenly distributed in time, space and variables, and carry errors from different sources. Data assimilation combines these observations with a short-range background forecast to produce a structurally complete, dynamically consistent analysis field, which then initializes the next forecast. Early AI forecast models mostly used ready-made analysis fields such as ERA5, leaving an assimilation gap between model training and real observations. FengWu-4DVar, FuXi-DA and LDA address this problem in model space, feature space and latent space respectively, representing three routes: differentiable variational assimilation, supervised fusion, and latent-space optimization.

Section Summary

Data assimilation connects AI weather models to real observations. The three routes differ mainly in whether observational constraints are imposed on physical states, learned features, or latent representations. The quality of the analysis field and the forecast improvement it brings are the two key threads for understanding the effectiveness of these assimilation methods.

Discussion

  • Which classical assimilation concepts are preserved by differentiable 4DVar, supervised feature fusion and latent-space variational optimization respectively?
  • How should simulated observations, real satellite observations and long-term cycling experiments each be used to evaluate an assimilation system?
  • Should physical consistency in latent space be verified through balance relations, conserved quantities, or subsequent forecast skill?

Suggested Reading Order

  1. 1Global deterministic forecasting: read FourCastNet, Pangu-Weather, GraphCast, FuXi and Aurora, comparing how they represent the global state and extend the task scope.
  2. 2Probabilistic forecasting and precipitation nowcasting: start with GenCast, FuXi-ENS and WeatherNext Cyclones, then follow DGMR → EarthFormer → PreDiff to understand the evolution from generative scenarios to space-time representations and latent diffusion, reading NowcastNet and DiffCast as contrasting methodological routes.
  3. 3Longer lead times and the ocean: use FuXi-S2S, QuickClim, NeuralGCM and UniCM to understand subseasonal and climate tasks, then learn ocean forecasting through OceanNet, WenHai and ORCA-DL.
  4. 4Evaluation and data assimilation: finish with WeatherBench 2, ClimateBench, the extreme-event evaluation, FengWu-4DVar, FuXi-DA and LDA, completing the forecast chain from observation to verification.
